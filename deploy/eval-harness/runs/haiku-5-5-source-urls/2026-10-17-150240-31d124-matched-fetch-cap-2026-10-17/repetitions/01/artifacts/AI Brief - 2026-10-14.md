## 🏛️ Policy, Safety & Society

### Anthropic asks the full D.C. Circuit to rehear the Pentagon's supply-chain-risk designation (October 13, 2026)

On October 13 Anthropic petitioned the D.C. Circuit for rehearing, asking either the original panel or the full bench to reconsider a September 25 decision that upheld the Defense Department's designation of the company as a supply-chain risk. The three-judge panel had split 2-1, with Judge Henderson dissenting. Anthropic's petition argues that the majority misread the statute by allowing the Secretary to label the company a national-security risk over pre-deployment training, a feature of all AI models, without any pre-designation process, and that the label was retaliation for Anthropic's public statements on AI safety. It also points to an August 27 ruling from a federal court in Northern California that found direct evidence of speech-based motivation in a parallel designation issued under a different statute. The label, applied in March, keeps the military and its contractors from using Claude in work with the agency. Coverage has not published the Pentagon's full stated rationale. Why it matters: the case tests whether a supply-chain statute written for foreign adversaries can reach a domestic lab over the usage limits it sets, and any ruling on the legal theory would not be confined to Anthropic.

### Anthropic says small open-weight models may soon self-replicate across IoT devices (October 12, 2026)

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research on October 12 arguing that open models of 30 billion parameters or fewer can already carry out many of the separate steps a self-replicating AI would need, even though they still fail when those steps are chained together. In the demonstration, the team split a 26-billion-parameter Gemma 4 variant across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes that cost about $55 each, reaching only 0.39 tokens per second. In a simplified test on a closed network of eight emulated Android nodes, where the hacking step was kept deliberately trivial, GLM-5.2 was the only open-weight model that completed full replication, reaching all eight nodes in four of five trials. Anthropic calls that an upper bound on real-world success, says it has no evidence of a working replicator in the wild, and projects that small models could reach GLM-5.2's level by the end of 2026 if they keep improving at their historical pace. The authors argue that such a worm would be hard to shut down because it has no central switch-off point, and that it would spread slowly, with a doubling time of days. Why it matters: the containment question shifts from datacenter access to the security of consumer IoT hardware, and Anthropic's suggested defence is to keep each infected device's onward spread rate below one.

### Anthropic reports that new distillation defenses cut harvested reasoning data by 90–99% (October 12, 2026)

Anthropic said on October 12 that three new defenses sharply reduce the reasoning data distillers can extract from Claude. On its own platform, the company measures reasoning-token extraction falling 10 to 100 times after its two-stage classifier was deployed. The first layer, preserved thinking, stops API accounts created on or after August 31, 2026 from editing Claude's prior context, which closes a route for moving protected reasoning blocks between conversations, models and accounts. The second is a two-stage classifier modelled on Anthropic's constitutional-classifier work, with a false-positive rate it puts below 0.1%. The third is tighter rate limits and bans; the company says it has banned tens of thousands of accounts tied to distillation campaigns. The trade-offs are real: some developers had to update integrations, benign requests are occasionally blocked, and blocked requests in covered categories have been billed since September 24. The post also accuses Zhipu of distilling US models' cyber capabilities while building GLM-5.3. In one internal comparison, a model trained on full reasoning transcripts scored 80.4% on SWE-bench Verified against 71.8% for one trained on summaries. Why it matters: reasoning traces are the most valuable material for a distiller, and the 90–99% figure is Anthropic's own measurement, not independently verified.

### Meta releases Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full (October 12, 2026) — Follow-up: the Muse agent first noted in the 2026-10-12 brief's Sierra Poppy item, which Amazon reportedly blocked for not identifying itself

Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full on October 12, calling them its first frontier-class models; both are slated to power the Muse agent and are positioned around personal and professional agent work rather than chat leaderboards. Muse Wonder is available through Meta's model API at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, with cached input at $0.20; Wonder Full is limited to Muse Code subscribers on the $50 and $200 monthly plans. Both accept text, image, video and audio input with a one-million-token context window, but output is text only. Artificial Analysis gives Wonder Full 56 on its Intelligence Index, level with Claude Sonnet 5.5 and behind only Claude Opus 5.5 at 58; standard Wonder scores 52. Meta's own scorecard favours Wonder Full on long-horizon work, with 57.5 on SWE-Marathon against 46.3 for Opus 5.5 and 13.1 on Harvey's legal agent benchmark against 4.2, while Opus 5.5 keeps leads on ProgramBench (70.5 to 59.0) and several terminal and computer-use tests. Meta's MemoryBench and FocusBench results are its own internal evaluations, and independent replication across the full suite had not been completed at launch. Why it matters: the competitive measure is moving from one-shot answer quality toward memory, sustained focus and cost per long-running task.

### Anthropic's internal source control moves to Scion, with merges above 30,000 a day (October 13, 2026)

Simon Willison, writing on October 13, summarised an Anthropic post describing a custom Git alternative, Scion, that the company built because its code-change rate had jumped. By his account, Anthropic averaged about 2,500 merged code changes a day in May 2026 and merged more than 30,000 a day by September. Willison guesses that the arrival of the Fable- and Mythos-class models drove the increase, and he labels that as a guess. Secondary coverage adds that a seven-person team built Scion and moved the monorepo off Git in five months, cutting infrastructure footprint 200-fold; I could not check that figure because Anthropic's own post was not retrievable in this run. Why it matters: once agent-written code volume outruns version-control and review tooling, the bottleneck moves from writing code to merging, reviewing and attributing it, which every team running coding agents will meet.

## 🏢 Industry, Deals & Strategy

### Microsoft unveils the Maia 300 inference accelerator (October 12, 2026)

Microsoft introduced Maia 300, the third generation of its in-house AI accelerator, and said the Maia family will scale to multi-gigawatt deployments from 2027, becoming the primary inference system for its own products and services. Each chip pairs eight stacks of HBM4 memory, delivering 18 TB/s of bandwidth within a 950-watt budget, and its compute chiplets are made on TSMC's N2P process in a layout that separates compute, memory controllers and I/O. A full 96-accelerator rack is specified at 167 kilowatts. Microsoft expects 40% more tokens per megawatt than leading GPU-based systems and up to seven times the rack-scale token throughput of Maia 200; it did not identify the GPU systems used for comparison, and the figures are company-reported. Reuters reported that the rollout could involve more than one million chips next year, and The Information had earlier reported talks with TSMC for more than 300,000 units; Microsoft has not disclosed volumes. Why it matters: a hyperscaler is trying to cut the cost of serving tokens and reduce its dependence on Nvidia and AMD, though analysts expect buyers to keep purchasing GPUs for mixed workloads.

### Amazon is reportedly in talks to buy Decart for about $7 billion (October 11, 2026)

The Wall Street Journal reported on October 11 that Amazon is negotiating to acquire Decart, an Israeli-founded AI startup, in a deal valued at around $7 billion. Reuters said it could not independently verify the report, and no agreement has been announced. Decart develops software that lets models run efficiently and move between chip families, including Nvidia GPUs, AWS Trainium and Google TPUs, along with world models such as Lucy and Oasis that generate and simulate real-time 3D environments. AWS gave Decart early access to Trainium3 in 2025. The reported price would be well above the nearly $4 billion valuation from Decart's May round, which Nvidia joined as an investor. The process has been unusually fluid: Anthropic reportedly pursued Decart at about $6 billion in August and walked away in September after due diligence, and reporting did not establish why. Why it matters: a cross-chip runtime lowers the cost of moving workloads off Nvidia, so the price suggests how much cloud providers now value software that makes non-Nvidia silicon usable.
